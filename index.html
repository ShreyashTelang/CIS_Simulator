THEORY & CRYPTANALYSIS

How Columnar Transposition Works

The Columnar Transposition Cipher belongs to the family of transposition (permutation) ciphers. Unlike substitution ciphers (e.g., Caesar, Monoalphabetic, VigenÃ¨re) which change the identities of characters, transposition ciphers preserve letter identities and only rearrange their positional indices.

Core Rule: The message is inscribed horizontally into a grid row by row, and then read out vertically column by column according to the alphabetical order of the keyword letters.

Key Alphabetical Ranking with Tie-Breaking

Given a keyword of length \(K\), each letter is assigned an integer rank from \(1\) to \(K\) based on its alphabetical position. When duplicate letters appear, ties are broken strictly from left to right.

  • Example 1 (ZEBRAS): Alphabetical order is A(1), B(2), E(3), R(4), S(5), Z(6). Ranks: [6, 3, 2, 4, 1, 5].
  • Example 2 (BANANA): Letters: B, A, N, A, N, A. Sorted: A(1), A(2), A(3), B(4), N(5), N(6). Breaking ties left-to-right assigns Pos 1 → 1, Pos 3 → 2, Pos 5 → 3, Pos 0 → 4, Pos 2 → 5, Pos 4 → 6. Resulting ranks: [4, 1, 5, 2, 6, 3].

Regular vs. Irregular Transposition

  • Regular (Padded): Null characters (traditionally 'X') are appended to pad the last row, forming a complete rectangle of size \(R \times K\). Every column has exactly \(R\) letters.
  • Irregular (Unpadded): No padding is added. If message length \(L\) is not a multiple of \(K\), the remainder \(rem = L \pmod K\) determines the column lengths: the first \(rem\) physical columns have length \(\lceil L/K \rceil\), while the remaining columns have length \(\lfloor L/K \rfloor\).

Why Columnar Transposition is Weak

Despite scattering letters across the message, columnar transposition suffers from critical structural vulnerabilities that allow cryptanalysts to break it without the key:

1. Complete Conservation of Unigram Frequencies:
Because no character is ever replaced, the relative frequency of every single letter in the ciphertext is identical to that of natural English plaintext. The letter 'E' remains the most frequent (~12.7%), followed by 'T' (~9.1%) and 'A' (~8.2%). An attacker computing the Index of Coincidence (IoC) immediately recognizes that the cipher is a transposition (\(IoC \approx 0.0667\)) rather than a polyalphabetic substitution (\(IoC \approx 0.0385\)).

2. Key Length Factoring (In Regular Transposition)

In regular (padded) transposition, the ciphertext length \(N\) is an exact multiple of the key length \(K\) (\(N = R \times K\)). By finding the integer factors of \(N\), the cryptanalyst narrows the possible key lengths down to just a handful of candidates. For example, if \(N = 35\), \(K\) must be either 5 or 7.

3. Anagramming and Digram Frequency Matching

Even when the keyword is unknown, an attacker who guesses a key length \(K\) can cut the ciphertext into \(K\) column strips. Because English contains well-known high-frequency digraphs (e.g., TH, HE, IN, ER, AN, RE) and impossible digraphs (e.g., QZ, JX, VK), the cryptanalyst compares pairs of columns row by row. By sliding candidate columns side by side and scoring bigram plausibility (a process known as multiple anagramming), the correct column permutation can be rapidly reconstructed in polynomial time.

Historical Significance

  • American Civil War: Both Union and Confederate signal corps used route and columnar transposition variations with pre-agreed keywords.
  • World War I (Ubchi Cipher): The German Army deployed an irregular double columnar transposition cipher named Ubchi. However, the French cryptanalyst Marcel Givierge broke it within months because German operators used predictable keys.
  • World War II (SOE Poem Codes): British Special Operations Executive (SOE) field agents were issued poems printed on silk cloths to derive daily columnar transposition keys. Leo Marks famously reformed this system to prevent anagramming attacks.

Modern Perspective: Transposition in AES

While pure transposition is insecure on its own, it remains a fundamental building block of modern symmetric cryptography. Claude Shannon formulated the principles of Confusion (obscuring the relationship between key and ciphertext) and Diffusion (spreading plaintext statistics across the ciphertext).

In modern ciphers like the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), substitution (the SubBytes step) is alternatingly combined with transposition: ShiftRows (cyclically permuting bytes within rows) and MixColumns (matrix transposition & diffusion across columns).
